# Chapter 1: The responsibility shift — worked answers

## The assistant that answered quickly

1. Output: assistant answers and staff handoffs during a bounded test. Desired but unmeasured outcome: learners completing relevant requirements correctly and without avoidable delay. Workload and business effectiveness are also unmeasured.
2. Timely-answer correctness: 72 / 90 = 80%. Timely-and-correct coverage of all questions: 72 / 100 = 72%. Eighteen timely answers were incorrect. Ten questions were handed to staff; the exercise supplies no correctness or completion outcome for those handoffs.
3. Ninety percent is only the proportion receiving a timely answer. It conflates speed with success and ignores wrong answers and downstream outcomes. Neither 80% nor 72% alone establishes readiness for a real deployment.
4. Investigate error severity and causes, representativeness of the test questions, source currency, inappropriate answers versus appropriate escalation, staff review burden, accessibility, data access, and consequences. A sample with known reviewed questions may not represent future real requests. Define risk-sensitive acceptance criteria before further use; do not invent a universal accuracy threshold.
5. The accountable onboarding business owner decides scope with the policy, operational, technical, and privacy/security owners as relevant. The assistant remains unable to change records. Do not give it new authority because its response-time score looks favorable. A defensible decision is to pause expansion, inspect errors, improve sources/controls, and retest with appropriate cases; a tightly bounded additional evaluation is also defensible if justified.

## Self-study scoring guide: 10 points

- 2: correctly distinguishes output and unmeasured outcome.
- 2: computes and labels both percentages and denominators.
- 2: explains why the response-time headline misleads.
- 2: proposes relevant investigation without fabricated evidence or universal thresholds.
- 2: identifies accountable ownership and retains authority boundaries.

Common mistakes: divide 72 by 90 and label it overall success; count staff handoffs as correct without evidence; recommend expanding solely because answers are fast; assume an AI-generated explanation is verified; invent a learner-start improvement. Give feedback by naming the unsupported inference and asking which evidence would support it.

## Transfer task: illustrative Harbor answer

Stakeholders: admitted learners and supporting employees. Desired outcome: complete requirements with fewer unresolved obstacles. Evidence: reported conflicting instructions and repeated checks; these are scenario facts, not a causal baseline. Unknowns: frequency, relative causes, and effect on readiness. Measurement responsibility: onboarding owner arranges authorized analysis with a defined cohort. Target follows baseline and constraints rather than an invented improvement rate.

Alternatives: clarify authoritative content and exception ownership; compare with bounded AI-assisted guidance. Dependency: policy owner must define and maintain current requirements; support must accept and resource escalation. AI may propose themes from an approved de-identified case set, with read-only access, evidence links, human review, and no live records or messaging. Fallback is manual review of the bounded case set. Review correction effort and missed cases as well as generation time.

The charter is ready for a first discussion when a receiving stakeholder can explain the outcome, evidence limits, decision owner, alternative, dependency, authority boundaries, and fallback. This is a practitioner acceptance test, not empirical validation of the course.

## Facilitation

Ask learners whether speed, correctness, safe escalation, or completion is being measured. Ask what an informed Operations colleague would challenge. Let a well-supported simpler or non-AI solution earn full credit. Keep student feedback respectful; assess the reasoning rather than confidence or agreement with the instructor.
